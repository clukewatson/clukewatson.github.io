What Happens to Branches When Banks Merge

For the merger simulation the bank deposit demand paper (Bank Competition for Neighborhood Deposits), I wanted to include an endogenous rule for which branches close and I wanted to base it on reality. How many, and which ones? For this, I use the FDIC Summary of Deposits and the FDIC structure change files from 2000 through 2025.

The short version: Distance

Almost everything is distance.

For every branch owned by either party to a merger, I measured how far it sat from the nearest branch belonging to the other party, as of the last Summary of Deposits before the deal closed. Below is the probability of being closed conditional on distance to the other merger-party.

Distance to nearest counterparty branch Target side Acquirer side
0–250 m 53% 24%
500 m–1 km 40% 22%
1–2 km 40% 22%
2–5 km 22% 12%
5–10 km 15% 5%
25 km + 8% 6%

If a target branch across the street from an acquirer branch is closed, then it has a 53% of being closed after three years. Another kilometer or two leads to a 40% closure rate, but after 25 there is no real difference in the closure rate with a random branch. Interestingly, we also see acquirer branch closures decay with distance from target branches. Condition on distance and the acquirer closes its own branches too, at roughly half the target rate rather than not at all. Thus, only pruning the target’s network will fail to accurately account for the total branch closures and likely get many wrong.

Closures are substitutes

The table above gives two marginal probabilities. Below, I show the the joint distribution of closures at within 250m of each other.

Outcome for a pair within 250 m Share
Neither closes 38%
Target closes only 47%
Acquirer closes only 10%
Both close 5%

Conditional on the target office closing, the acquirer’s nearby office is less likely to close, 9% against 21%. That is what you would expect if the merged bank is keeping one office and shutting the other, which is presumably what is going on.

Three things that nearly fooled me

The headline result is tidy. Getting to it was not, and the detours are the more useful part of the exercise.

The branch identifier. The Summary of Deposits gives every branch a UNINUMBR and also a BRNUM, which is the branch’s number within its owning bank. BRNUM is the obvious key and it is wrong. When Wells Fargo bought Wachovia, I can follow 3,116 of Wachovia’s 3,338 branches into Wells Fargo’s 2010 filing using UNINUMBR — and every single one of those branches got a new BRNUM. A panel keyed on the bank-branch number reads that acquisition as 3,338 closures followed by 3,116 openings. If you are working with this data, this is the first thing to get right.

There is a catch. UNINUMBR is blank for 18% of rows in 2000, 7% in 2010, and 0% from 2011 onward. The blanks are not random: they cluster inside particular banks, so where any of a bank’s branches are missing an identifier the median bank is missing two thirds of them. That also means the usual fallback of matching on address does not help much, because there is no identified branch at the same address to match to.

Sales cannot be separated from noise. I wanted to split “closed” from “sold to a third party,” since a branch sold to a rival is very different for competition than a branch shut down. I infer a sale from the branch showing up under a different holding company. The problem is that holding company identifiers change on their own. Banks that are never party to any recorded combination — no merger, no failure, no charter reorganization — show their holding company identifier changing for 4.06% of branch-years per year. For banks that are merger parties, it is 4.14%.

Those are the same number. Over three years that is a floor of roughly 12%, which is larger than any merger-related sale share I measured. So the sale margin is not identified here. The closure margin is clean and large; the sale margin I can only bound.

Silicon Valley Bank’s main office. Late in this I looked at which closures accounted for the most deposits and found that the top ten closures held 78% of all closed deposits. The largest single one was Silicon Valley Bank’s main office, booking $120 billion.

A main office in this data books deposits for the whole institution, not for the people who walk into that building. It is an accounting location, not a retail catchment. Five of the eight largest “closures” in my sample were main offices. Drop them and the deposit share closed falls from 28% to 11%, and the top-ten concentration falls from 78% to 21%.

I nearly calibrated a rule against that 28%. It would have been a number about where large banks book deposits, dressed up as a fact about branch closures.

Is any of it predictable?

Here is the honest part. At the level of an individual county, closures look close to random, and I wanted to know how much of that is my rule being bad versus the outcome being genuinely noisy.

Branch closure is a coin flip with a known bias. Even a rule that knew every branch’s true probability would still miss the realized count, because a coin flip is a coin flip. You can decompose the variance in the county-level count into the part a rule could in principle explain and the part that is irreducible:

Component Value
Variance in closures across counties 1.99
Irreducible binomial variance 0.71
Best \(R^2\) any rule could achieve 0.65
\(R^2\) from distance and relative size 0.28

So 35% of the county-level variation is unreachable by construction. Of the 65% that is knowable, distance and relative branch size recover a little under half.

That sounds discouraging until you remember what a simulation needs. It does not need to predict any particular county. It needs the expectation right so that closures aggregate correctly, and on that the rule is close to exact:

Predicted closures in a county Counties Predicted Actual
under 0.25 111 0.24 0.24
0.25–0.5 323 0.37 0.43
0.5–1 403 0.72 0.74
1–2 252 1.38 1.40
2–4 84 2.67 2.64

Being wrong about individual counties while being right on average is the correct behavior here, not a defect.

The rule I ended up with

In a county where both banks have branches, with \(n_A\) and \(n_T\) branches each:

\[ n_{\text{closed}} \;=\; 0.6 \times \min(n_A, n_T) \]

or equivalently, the number surviving is

\[ n_{\text{post}} \;=\; (n_A + n_T) - 0.6 \times \min(n_A, n_T) \;=\; \max(n_A,n_T) + 0.4 \times \min(n_A,n_T). \]

About 60% of the smaller network goes. Not all of it, which is what “the acquirer absorbs the target locally” would imply and which overshoots by two thirds. Not none of it either.

The form is worth writing out carefully because the natural-looking version is wrong. I first wrote it as \(\max - 0.5\min\), which subtracts from the larger network instead of from the combined total, and closes two and a half times too many branches. That version does worse than assuming nothing closes at all.

For picking which branches, a two-line rule does about as well as anything: take the target’s branches before the acquirer’s, and within that take the smallest by deposits. That identifies 56% of the branches that actually closed, against 38% for picking at random and 57% for a rule that uses the full empirical probability table. Sorting by distance instead is barely better than random, which surprised me given that distance drives the rate so strongly. The county count already encodes the rate; what is left for the selection step is which office within the county, and size answers that better than proximity does.

One caveat on the selection rule. When exactly one office of a nearby pair closes, it is the smaller one about two thirds of the time. But when the two are similar in size, or far apart, it is 54% — a coin flip. So “the smaller one closes” is a decent default and a bad certainty.

What this cannot tell you

The timing is annual. The Summary of Deposits is a June 30 snapshot, so “within one year” really means “by the next June,” and I cannot separate a merger’s effect from anything else that happened in the same twelve months.

I cannot distinguish an antitrust-mandated divestiture from a voluntary one. Neither data source flags them, so the numbers blend the two.

I cannot see closures that were announced and then abandoned, only ones that happened.

And the whole thing is a reduced-form correlation, not a causal closure rule. It tells you which branches were closed historically. Using it inside a simulation assumes that the same rule carries over to the counterfactual merger you are simulating, which is an assumption, not a finding.

The last one is worth sitting with. I started this wanting to replace an assumption with a fact and ended up replacing a bad assumption with a better-calibrated one. That is still progress, but it is a different thing than it felt like at the outset.
